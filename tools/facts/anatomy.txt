A cat's eyes are very large for the size of its head, which helps them gather light in dim conditions.
Cats cannot see in total darkness, but they can see with about one sixth of the light a person needs.
The tapetum lucidum is a mirror-like layer behind a cat's retina that bounces light back through it a second time.
The glow of a cat's eyes in headlights or a camera flash is light reflecting off the tapetum lucidum.
Humans have no tapetum lucidum, which is one reason cats see so much better than people at night.
The tapetum aids night vision but scatters some light, which is one reason a cat's eyesight is less sharp than ours.
A cat's pupils are vertical slits that open almost round in the dark and narrow to a thin line in bright sun.
A slit pupil can change the amount of light entering the eye far more than a round pupil like a human's.
Vertical slit pupils are typical of small ambush hunters that stalk close to the ground, such as cats.
Scientists think the vertical slit shape also helps a cat judge distance precisely when it is stalking prey.
In very bright light a cat's slit pupil can close in the middle, leaving two tiny openings at the top and bottom.
The iris of a cat's eye has two sets of muscles, one that narrows the pupil and one that widens it.
A cat's retina has many rod cells, which work in dim light and detect movement, and fewer colour-sensing cones.
Rod cells cannot tell colours apart, so in dim light a cat, like a person, sees mostly in shades of grey.
Cats are not colour blind, but they see fewer colours than people do, and blues and yellows show up best.
Reds and pinks are thought to look dull or greyish to a cat, while blue and yellow-green stand out clearly.
A cat's eyesight is much less sharp than a person's: roughly speaking, 20/20 human vision is 20/100 or worse for a cat.
Cats struggle to focus on anything closer than about 25 centimetres, so up close they rely on whiskers and smell.
A cat's field of view is about 200 degrees, wider than a human's roughly 180 degrees.
A cat's eyes face forward like a person's, giving it good binocular vision for judging the distance of a pounce.
A cat's eyes are built for the low light of dawn and dusk, when its natural prey is most active.
Cats have a third eyelid, called the nictitating membrane, that sweeps across the eye from the inner corner.
The third eyelid normally stays tucked out of sight in the inner corner and helps protect and moisten the eye.
The third eyelid also contains a gland that produces a good share of a cat's tears.
Tears from a cat's eyes drain through small ducts into its nose, as they do in people.
A cat's retina has a central area packed with cells for detail, but no true fovea like the one in a human eye.
The clear cornea at the front of a cat's eye covers a larger share of the eyeball than a human cornea does.
Cats are excellent at spotting movement, and a mouse that freezes can be harder for a cat to see than one that runs.
A 2014 study found that a cat's lens lets through some ultraviolet light, so cats may see patterns invisible to people.
Cats can hear sounds up to around 64 kilohertz, more than three times the upper limit of human hearing.
A cat's hearing range is one of the widest of any mammal, from deep rumbles to very high ultrasonic squeaks.
Cats hear higher pitches than dogs can, and far higher than people can.
At the low end a cat's hearing is roughly similar to a person's. The big difference is at the high end.
Each of a cat's ears is moved by around 30 small muscles, compared with about six in a human ear.
A cat can swivel each ear on its own and turn it through about 180 degrees to home in on a sound.
The outer ear flap of a cat is called the pinna, and its cone shape funnels sound into the ear canal.
A cat's ear flap is supported by cartilage, which is why it is both stiff and bendable.
A cat can pinpoint where a sound came from to within about five degrees.
When a cat tilts its head toward a sound, it is comparing tiny differences in timing and loudness between its ears.
The small fold of skin on the outer edge of a cat's ear is called Henry's pocket, or the cutaneous marginal pouch.
Nobody knows for sure what Henry's pocket is for. It may help detect high sounds or let the ear fold more easily.
A cat's ear canal is L-shaped, running down the side of the head and then turning inward toward the eardrum.
The bony chamber of a cat's middle ear is divided into two compartments by a thin wall of bone.
The smallest bones in a cat's body are the three tiny bones of the middle ear that carry sound to the inner ear.
A cat's inner ear contains the vestibular system, fluid-filled canals that tell the cat which way is up.
The semicircular canals in a cat's inner ear sense rotation of the head in three different planes.
Cats can hear the ultrasonic calls that mice and other rodents use to communicate with each other.
Whiskers are stiff, specialised hairs called vibrissae, rooted about three times deeper in the skin than ordinary hairs.
The word vibrissae comes from the Latin word for vibrate, because whiskers quiver as they pick up movement in the air.
A typical cat has about 24 whiskers on its muzzle, 12 on each side, arranged in four rows.
The top two rows of a cat's muzzle whiskers can move independently of the bottom two rows.
The base of each whisker sits in a blood-filled capsule packed with nerve endings, which amplifies tiny movements.
There are no nerves inside the whisker hair itself. All the sensing happens at the root.
When a whisker tip touches something, the bend is sensed at the root and tells the cat its shape and texture.
Whiskers are so sensitive that they register changes in air currents around an object a cat has not even touched.
Whiskers help a cat sense the position of prey in the dark, when its eyes cannot focus on something so close.
A cat's muzzle whiskers spread roughly as wide as its body, which helps it judge whether it can fit through a gap.
Besides the muzzle, cats have whiskers above the eyes, on the chin, on the cheeks and on the back of each front leg.
The whiskers on the back of a cat's front legs are called carpal whiskers. They help the cat feel prey held in its paws.
The whiskers above a cat's eyes are called superciliary whiskers, and touching them triggers a protective blink.
The small whiskers on a cat's cheeks are called genal whiskers, and those on the chin are mandibular whiskers.
The puffy area on each side of a cat's upper lip where the whiskers grow is called the whisker pad or mystacial pad.
Small muscles at the base of the whiskers let a cat sweep them forward or pull them back flat against its face.
Whiskers shed and regrow naturally, usually one or two at a time, like other hairs.
Whiskers are much thicker than ordinary hairs, which is part of what makes them stiff enough to sense by touch.
A large part of the touch-sensing area of a cat's brain is devoted to signals from its whiskers.
Every cat's nose has a unique pattern of ridges and bumps, much like a human fingerprint.
The hairless skin on the tip of a cat's nose is called the nose leather, or planum nasale.
A cat's upper lip is divided by a vertical groove below the nose, called the philtrum.
A cat's sense of smell is far keener than a human's, with many more scent-detecting cells lining its nose.
A cat's nasal passages are lined with scroll-like bones called turbinates that warm and filter the air it breathes.
A slightly damp nose helps a cat smell, because scent molecules dissolve in the moisture and reach the receptors.
Cats have a second scent organ, the vomeronasal or Jacobson's organ, in the roof of the mouth.
The Jacobson's organ opens into the mouth through two tiny ducts just behind the upper front teeth.
A cat that sniffs something and then holds its mouth slightly open is drawing scent up into its Jacobson's organ.
That open-mouthed sniffing face is called the flehmen response, from a German word for baring the upper teeth.
The Jacobson's organ is tuned to pheromones, the chemical signals that cats leave for one another.
The Jacobson's organ sends its signals to the brain along its own pathway, separate from ordinary smell.
Cats share the Jacobson's organ with horses, snakes and many other animals, but humans have only a trace of one.
A cat's tongue is covered in hundreds of tiny backward-facing spines called papillae, made of keratin like its claws.
The papillae are why a cat's lick feels like sandpaper. They work as a built-in comb when the cat grooms.
The spines on a cat's tongue are about 2 millimetres long and shaped like tiny hollow scoops at the tip.
The hollow tips of the papillae wick saliva deep into the fur during grooming, cleaning the cat right down to the skin.
Engineers who studied cat tongue papillae in 2018 built a hairbrush based on their design.
The backward-pointing spines on a cat's tongue also help it rasp meat off bones.
Cats have far fewer taste buds than people, around 470 compared with roughly 9,000 in a human.
Cats cannot taste sweet flavours. The gene for the sweet taste receptor does not work in cats.
Cats are sensitive to bitter tastes and have several working bitter taste receptor genes.
Saliva spread on the fur by grooming evaporates and cools the cat, much as sweat cools a person.
A cat drinks by touching the tip of its tongue to the water, then pulling it up fast to draw a column of water upward.
The cat snaps its jaws shut on the column of water just before gravity pulls it back down.
A cat does not suck water up. It relies on the momentum of the water column its tongue pulls into the air.
A 2010 study of lapping found that cats lap about four times a second, taking in a tiny amount of water each time.
When a cat laps, the tip of its tongue moves at close to 80 centimetres per second.
Unlike dogs, cats do not plunge or scoop with a cupped tongue. They only brush the surface with the tongue tip.
Because only the tip of the tongue touches the water, a cat keeps its chin dry when it drinks.
Kittens have 26 milk teeth, and adult cats have 30 permanent teeth.
An adult cat has 12 incisors, 4 canines, 10 premolars and 4 molars.
An adult cat has 16 teeth in its upper jaw and 14 in its lower jaw.
A kitten's set of milk teeth has no molars. Those arrive only with the adult teeth.
A cat's four long, pointed canine teeth are the longest teeth in its mouth and are built for gripping and piercing.
The carnassial teeth, a large upper premolar and lower molar on each side, work like scissor blades to shear meat.
The upper carnassial tooth is the fourth premolar, and the lower one is the first molar.
Behind each canine there is a gap in a cat's tooth row that leaves room for the opposite canine to close.
A cat's teeth have no broad grinding surfaces, so it slices food into pieces and swallows them whole.
The enamel on a cat's teeth is the hardest substance in its body, as it is in people.
A cat's jaw moves almost only up and down. It cannot grind side to side the way a human jaw can.
A cat's lower jaw is two separate bones joined at the chin by a flexible joint, not fused into one as in a human.
A cat's skull has wide cheekbones that anchor its powerful jaw muscles.
The small incisors at the front of a cat's mouth are used for nibbling and for picking at fur when grooming.
The roof of a cat's mouth has ridges called rugae that help hold food in place.
A cat has roughly 230 to 250 bones, depending on the length of its tail, compared with 206 in an adult human.
Most of a cat's extra bones are in its spine and tail. Cats have many more vertebrae than people do.
A cat has seven neck bones, the same number as a human and a giraffe.
A cat's back has 13 chest vertebrae and 7 lower-back vertebrae, compared with 12 and 5 in a human.
Three fused vertebrae called the sacrum connect a cat's spine to its pelvis.
A cat has 13 pairs of ribs, one more pair than a human.
A cat's tail is an extension of its spine, made of small bones called caudal vertebrae.
A typical cat tail contains around 18 to 23 bones.
A cat's collarbone is tiny and free-floating, attached to the rest of the skeleton only by muscle.
Because its collarbone is not fixed to other bones, a cat can usually squeeze through any gap its head fits through.
A cat's shoulder blades are attached to its body mostly by muscle, so its shoulders move freely as it walks.
Loose shoulders let a cat take long strides, with each front paw landing almost directly in front of the other.
The discs between a cat's vertebrae are especially thick and elastic, which gives the spine its remarkable flexibility.
A cat's spine is flexible enough for it to curl into a tight circle and tuck its nose under its tail.
The hyoid, a small bone that supports the tongue and voice box, is fully hardened bone in a domestic cat.
The joint that looks like a backward-bending knee on a cat's hind leg is actually its ankle, called the hock.
Cats have kneecaps on their hind legs, just as people do.
The joint partway down a cat's front leg is its wrist, so a standing cat is really standing on its fingers.
The two bones of a cat's forearm can rotate around each other, so a cat can turn its forepaws partly palm-up.
A cat's hind legs are longer and more muscular than its front legs, which is where its jumps get their power.
A cat's body has more than 500 muscles, which power everything from a whisker twitch to a leap.
A cat's skull has very large eye sockets and a short snout compared with most other carnivores.
Most cats have 18 toes: five on each front paw and four on each back paw.
A cat's dew claw, the fifth toe on each front paw, sits higher on the inside of the leg and stays off the ground.
Cats normally have no dew claw on their back paws.
The dew claw on each front leg works a little like a thumb, helping a cat grip prey and climb.
A cat's claws are sheathed at rest. It has to tense a muscle to extend them, not to pull them in.
Walking with the claws retracted keeps them sharp and lets a cat move almost silently.
Cats are digitigrade, meaning they walk on their toes with the heel and wrist raised off the ground.
A cat's claws grow from the last bone of each toe and are made of keratin, the same protein as human fingernails.
Inside each claw is a pink core called the quick, which carries blood vessels and nerves.
Cat claws grow in layers, and the old outer layer is shed as a hollow husk when a new claw grows underneath.
The back claws are usually blunter than the front ones, worn down by walking and not sharpened by scratching.
Cats sweat mainly through their paws. The pads contain sweat glands, and a stressed cat can leave damp pawprints.
A cat's paw pads are tough but packed with nerve endings that sense texture, pressure and vibration.
The skin of a cat's paw pads is much thicker than the skin elsewhere on its body.
A cat's paw pads help absorb the shock of landing from a jump.
The small pads under a cat's toes are called digital pads.
The big central pad on a cat's paw is called the metacarpal pad on the front feet and the metatarsal pad on the back.
A small extra pad higher up the back of each front leg, the carpal pad, helps a cat grip when it lands or brakes.
Cats have scent glands between their toes, so their paws leave a scent as well as a print.
Studies have found that many cats favour one front paw. Females tend to favour the right and males the left.
A cat's print shows four toe pads and a larger central pad, and normally no claw marks, since the claws are sheathed.
Because a cat's shoulders attach by muscle rather than bone, muscle cushions the shock when it lands from a jump.
A cat's coat usually has three kinds of hair: long guard hairs, medium awn hairs, and soft, fine down hairs.
The guard hairs form the outer layer of the coat and shed water, while the downy undercoat traps a layer of warm air.
Cat hair grows in clusters: each follicle opening usually holds one thick guard hair and several finer hairs.
Cats shed all year round, but shedding is heaviest in spring and autumn as the coat changes with the length of the day.
Each hair in a cat's coat grows for a while, rests, and then falls out as a new hair pushes up beneath it.
Each hair follicle has a tiny muscle that can make the hair stand on end. This reflex is called piloerection.
Oil glands in a cat's skin coat the fur with a film that helps keep water out.
Cats do have sweat glands over most of the body, but these make an oily, scented secretion rather than cooling sweat.
A cat's skin is loosely attached to the muscle beneath, so a cat can twist and slide inside its own skin.
A thin sheet of muscle under the skin lets a cat twitch its back to shake off a fly without moving its legs.
The loose flap of skin and fat along a cat's lower belly is the primordial pouch, a normal feature at any weight.
The primordial pouch may protect the belly in a fight and give the cat room to stretch out at a full run.
Cats have scent glands in the skin of their cheeks, chin, lips, forehead and the base of their tail.
The scent gland at the base of a cat's tail is called the supracaudal gland.
The sparse fur in front of each ear is normal. This thin patch is called the preauricular region.
Fur insulates against heat as well as cold, which is one reason a cat can lie comfortably in strong sun.
Both male and female cats have nipples, usually six to eight, arranged in two rows along the belly.
An average domestic cat is about 46 centimetres long from nose to tail base, with a tail of around 30 centimetres.
A typical domestic cat stands about 23 to 25 centimetres tall at the shoulder.
Male domestic cats are usually a little larger and heavier than females.
A cat's normal body temperature is about 38 to 39 degrees Celsius, which would count as a fever in a person.
A resting cat's heart beats about 140 to 220 times a minute, roughly twice as fast as an adult human heart.
A cat at rest takes about 20 to 30 breaths a minute, compared with 12 to 20 for an adult human.
Cats have three blood types: A, B and AB. Type A is by far the most common.
Cats have no universal donor blood type. Even type A blood cannot safely be given to every cat.
A cat's red blood cells are smaller than a human's and live only about 70 days, against roughly 120 days in a person.
Most of a cat's body cannot sweat, so it loses heat through its paw pads, by panting and by grooming.
Cats rarely pant. Unlike dogs, they mostly keep cool by resting in the shade and licking their coat.
A cat's liver burns protein for energy at a steady high rate and cannot turn this down, unlike a dog's or a human's.
A cat's body cannot make enough of the amino acid taurine, so it must come from the animal tissue the cat eats.
Cats cannot turn the beta-carotene in plants into vitamin A. Their bodies need the ready-made form found in animals.
Unlike people, cats make almost no vitamin D in their skin from sunlight and rely on their food for it.
A cat's kidneys can concentrate urine very strongly, so a cat can get by on less water than a dog of the same size.
A cat's gut is short for its size, as expected for an animal that digests meat rather than plants.
A cat's brain weighs about 25 to 30 grams, less than one percent of its body weight.
The surface of a cat's brain is folded like a human's, unlike the smooth brains of rats and mice.
A cat's cerebral cortex, the outer layer of the brain, holds roughly 250 to 300 million nerve cells.
The cerebellum, the part of the brain that coordinates movement and balance, is well developed in cats.
A large part of a cat's brain is devoted to processing information from its senses, especially sight.
The part of a cat's brain that handles smell, the olfactory bulb, is large relative to the size of the brain.
Much of what science knows about how brains process vision came from Hubel and Wiesel's studies of cat brains.
The righting reflex lets a falling cat twist in midair so that it lands on its feet.
A falling cat rights its head and front half first, guided by its inner ear and eyes, and its back half follows.
Cats twist in midair by bending at the waist and rotating the front and back halves of the body in opposite directions.
A cat needs nothing to push against to right itself in the air. The twist comes entirely from its own body.
A cat's flexible spine and free-floating collarbone are what make the midair twist possible.
A cat does not need its tail to land on its feet. The midair twist comes from its spine, with the tail only fine-tuning.
A cat can right itself in a fall of only about 30 centimetres, in a fraction of a second.
The vestibular system in a cat's inner ear senses which way is down even when the cat cannot see.
Once a falling cat is the right way up, it spreads its legs out to slow itself, a little like a parachute.
A cat's terminal velocity, the top speed it reaches in a long fall, is around 100 kilometres per hour.
A cat's top falling speed is roughly half that of a falling person, because a cat is light for its surface area.
A cat lands from a fall with its legs bent and its back arched, which spreads the shock across its body.
A domestic cat can sprint at about 48 kilometres per hour (30 miles per hour) over a short distance.
At full sprint a cat is faster than the fastest human sprinter ever recorded.
Cats are sprinters, not distance runners. Their muscles are rich in fast-twitch fibres that tire quickly.
A healthy adult cat can leap up to about five or six times its own body length in a single jump.
From a standing start a cat can jump straight up to around 1.5 metres, several times its own height.
A cat crouches before a jump to stretch the muscles of its hind legs and store energy like a spring.
When a cat leaps, its hind legs provide nearly all the push, while the front legs steer and take the landing.
A cat jumping down from a height lands front paws first, then brings its hind paws forward.
A cat climbs a tree by hooking in its curved claws and pushing upward with its powerful hind legs.
Cats get stuck up trees because their curved claws grip on the way up but cannot hook on when the cat faces down.
To get down safely a cat has to back down the trunk tail first, which many cats are reluctant to do.
Unlike squirrels, cats cannot rotate their hind feet to point backward, so they cannot run down a tree head first.
Cats walk with a pacing gait, moving both legs on one side and then both on the other, as camels and giraffes do.
A walking cat places each back paw almost exactly in the spot where the front paw on the same side just stood.
Stepping the back paw into the front paw's spot keeps a stalking cat quiet, since that ground is already tested.
When a cat trots, diagonally opposite legs move together. At a full gallop both hind legs push off almost at once.
At a full gallop there are moments when all four of a cat's paws are off the ground at once.
When a cat runs flat out, its flexible spine stretches and compresses with each bound, which lengthens its stride.
A cat's tail works as a counterweight, helping it balance when it walks along a narrow fence or shelf.
Purring comes from the muscles of the voice box twitching 25 to 150 times a second, opening and closing the airway.
A cat purrs while breathing in and while breathing out, which is why the sound seems continuous.
A 2023 study found pads of tissue in a cat's vocal folds that may let it purr with very little muscle effort.
Meows, hisses and chirps are made by air passing over the vocal folds in a cat's larynx, much like human speech sounds.
A hiss is an unvoiced sound: the cat forces air out through its open mouth without vibrating its vocal folds.
A cat's vocal folds sit inside the larynx at the top of the windpipe, just as a person's do.
