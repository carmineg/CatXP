Cats purr at roughly 25 to 150 vibrations per second, and they keep purring both as they breathe in and out.
A purr is made in the voice box, where muscles twitch to open and close the airway as the cat breathes.
Cats purr when content, but they also purr when frightened, in pain or giving birth.
Many vets think purring can be self-soothing, since cats also purr when they are hurt or stressed.
Some researchers have suggested the vibration range of a purr might help bones and tissue heal, but this is unproven.
A study found cats hide a high-pitched cry inside their purr when asking for food, and people find it hard to ignore.
A purr is as much felt as heard. The vibration travels through the whole body, so a lap cat seems to hum.
Not every cat purrs loudly. Some purr so softly that only a hand on the chest can detect it.
Cats sometimes purr when they meet a cat they know well or while grooming each other.
Adult cats rarely meow at each other. Meowing is mostly something they do for people.
Cats seem to learn which meows get a response from their owner and use those more often.
Feral cats that grow up without people meow far less than pet cats do.
A meow can mean many things: a greeting, a request for food, a protest or a demand for attention.
In a silent meow the cat opens its mouth as if to meow but makes no sound a person can hear.
A study found people are poor at guessing what a meow means, though those with lots of cat experience did better.
A classic study in the 1940s sorted house cat sounds into 16 patterns, from quiet murmurs to loud shrieks.
A study found people rate the meows of pet cats as more pleasant than the calls of their wild relatives.
The jaw chattering a cat makes while watching birds is thought to show excitement at prey it cannot reach.
One theory holds that chattering at prey is a rehearsal of the quick bite a cat uses to kill it.
Chirps and trills are short rising sounds cats use as a friendly greeting or to get a person to follow them.
Cats learn the trill from their mothers, who use it to call them along. Adults use it as a friendly hello.
Hissing is a defensive sound. The cat forces air out through an open mouth to warn a threat to back off.
A hiss is often a bluff. Most cats would rather frighten a rival away than fight it.
Growling is a low warning that a cat feels threatened or is guarding something, such as food or a toy.
Yowling, a long drawn-out cry, is used by unneutered cats looking for a mate and by cats arguing over territory.
Cat fights are loud but usually short. Most disputes end with one cat backing off before anyone is hurt.
Play fights between cats are mostly quiet. Real fights come with hissing, growling and screaming.
In a play fight the cats take turns chasing. In a real fight one cat is clearly the chaser and the other wants out.

A tail tucked low or wrapped tightly around the body signals a nervous or unhappy cat.

Forward-facing ears show a cat is interested and relaxed.
Ears flattened to the sides and back, sometimes called airplane ears, mean a cat is scared, annoyed or ready to fight.

A direct, unblinking stare is a challenge between cats, so a cat that looks away is being polite, not rude.
