A cat's pregnancy lasts about nine weeks, usually between 63 and 67 days.
A female cat is called a queen, and a typical litter has three to five kittens.
Litters of just one kitten or of more than eight both happen, though three to five is usual.
One litter can have more than one father, because a queen may mate with several males while in heat.
Kittens in a single litter having different fathers is called superfecundation.
A pregnant queen looks for a quiet, dark, hidden spot to give birth, such as a wardrobe or under a bed.
A mother cat may carry her kittens one by one to a new hiding place if the first nest is disturbed.
Kittens are born inside a thin membrane sac, which the mother usually breaks open and licks away.
A mother cat licks each newborn clean, which also helps start its breathing, and she usually eats the placenta.
Each kitten has its own placenta, and the queen normally passes one after each kitten is born.
A queen usually delivers her kittens between ten minutes and an hour apart, though she can pause labour for hours.
Newborn kittens weigh around 100 grams (about 3.5 ounces), roughly the weight of a bar of soap.
Kittens are born with their eyes sealed shut and their ear canals closed, so at first they can neither see nor hear.
A kitten's eyes usually open when it is between one and two weeks old.
Kittens' eyes are blue when they first open, and their adult eye colour comes in gradually over the following months.
Kittens' ear canals open during the second week of life, and their hearing sharpens over the following weeks.
The umbilical cord stump on a newborn kitten dries up and falls off within about three days.
Newborn kittens cannot keep themselves warm, so they huddle against their mother and each other for body heat.
Newborn kittens sleep for about 90 percent of the day, waking mainly to nurse.
Newborn kittens nurse every two to three hours around the clock in their first week or two.
The mother's first milk, called colostrum, passes on antibodies that protect the kittens in their first weeks.
Very young kittens cannot go to the toilet by themselves, so their mother licks them to make them go.
Kittens can purr when they are only a few days old, often while they nurse.
Kittens can crawl within their first few days but usually do not walk steadily until about three weeks old.
A healthy kitten roughly doubles its birth weight by two weeks old.
By about ten weeks old, a kitten often weighs around a kilogram (just over two pounds), ten times its birth weight.
A kitten's milk teeth begin to come through at about two to three weeks of age.
Kittens have 26 milk teeth. Adult cats have 30, fewer than a dog's 42 or a person's 32.
Kittens lose their milk teeth and grow their adult set between about three and six months of age.
Kittens start to try solid food at about four weeks old and are usually fully weaned by eight weeks.
Kittens usually start using a litter tray at around three to four weeks old, often by copying their mother.
Kittens begin grooming themselves at about four weeks old, and littermates soon start grooming each other.
By five to six weeks, kittens are running, climbing and pouncing on their littermates.
The key socialisation window for kittens runs from about two to seven weeks of age.
Kittens handled gently by people during their first weeks tend to grow into more confident, friendly adults.
Play-fighting with littermates teaches kittens how hard they can bite and swipe before the game stops.
A mother cat carries her kittens by the scruff of the neck, and kittens go limp and quiet when held this way.
Kittens should stay with their mother and littermates until at least eight weeks old. Many breeders wait until twelve.
A kitten's protection from its mother's milk fades at around eight to twelve weeks, which is when vaccinations begin.
Kittens usually have their first vaccinations at about eight or nine weeks old, with a second dose a month later.
Many kittens have a pot belly from roundworms, and a wormy kitten can look well fed while really being thin.
Roundworms are the most common worm in kittens, which can catch them through their mother's milk.
Kittens are usually wormed every two weeks from about three weeks old, then monthly until they are six months old.
Pale gums in a kitten can be a sign of anaemia caused by a heavy load of fleas or worms.
Swallowed string or thread is a common kitten emergency, because it can cut into the gut as it is dragged along.
A growing kitten needs roughly two to three times as many calories per kilogram as an adult cat.
Kitten food is richer in calories, protein and certain minerals than adult food because kittens grow so fast.
Kittens are usually fed three or four small meals a day until about six months old, then two meals a day.
Kittens separated from their mother too early need a kitten milk replacer. Cow's milk does not have the right nutrients.
A queen nursing a large litter may need two to three times her normal daily calories, so she is often fed kitten food.
A pregnant cat's nipples turn pinker and more prominent at about three weeks, a change known as pinking up.
A vet can often feel kittens in a pregnant queen's belly at three to four weeks, and an ultrasound can see them earlier.
A queen can come back into heat and become pregnant again while she is still nursing her kittens.
An unspayed cat can have two or even three litters a year.
Female cats can come into heat and become pregnant as young as four to six months old, before they are fully grown.
Most cats reach sexual maturity at around six months old, and females often get there before males.
Male cats, called toms, can father kittens from around six months of age.
Unspayed female cats come into heat again and again through spring and summer, roughly every two to three weeks.
A heat lasts about a week on average, and if the cat does not mate, the cycle starts again a couple of weeks later.
A female cat in heat does not usually bleed. Instead she becomes very vocal, restless and unusually affectionate.
Cats are induced ovulators, meaning the act of mating itself triggers the release of eggs.
Day length drives the cat's breeding season, so indoor cats living under artificial light may cycle all year round.
Female cats do not go through menopause. An unspayed queen can keep having kittens into old age, though litters shrink.
Spaying removes a female cat's ovaries and usually her uterus, which ends heat cycles and prevents pregnancy.
Spaying a cat before her first heat greatly lowers her risk of mammary (breast) cancer later in life.
Unspayed cats can develop pyometra, a serious infection of the womb, which spaying prevents completely.
Neutering a male cat removes the testicles. It is a short operation and most cats go home the same day.
Neutering a tom usually reduces roaming, fighting and the spraying of strong-smelling urine.
Most vets neuter cats at around four to six months old, and many rescue centres do it from about eight weeks.
Neutered cats need fewer calories than before, so their portions are usually reduced to prevent weight gain.
Neutered cats live longer on average than unneutered ones, partly because they roam and fight less.
Cats go through an adolescence from about six months to two years, physically mature but still bold and playful.
Kittens are usually considered adults at about one year old, but some large cats keep filling out until three or four.
A cat's growth plates, the soft zones at the ends of its bones, finish hardening at around one to one and a half years.
The idea that one cat year equals seven human years is a myth. A one-year-old cat is more like a 15-year-old person.
A two-year-old cat is roughly equivalent to a 24-year-old human, and each year after that adds about four human years.
By that reckoning, a 10-year-old cat is about 56 in human years, and a 15-year-old is about 76.
A cat reaching 20 is roughly like a person reaching 96.
Cats are generally classed as mature from about 7, senior from about 11 and geriatric from about 15 years old.
The average lifespan of a well-cared-for pet cat is about 12 to 15 years, and many live well into their late teens.
Cats living only outdoors often survive just a few years, while indoor cats commonly live well into their teens.
Indoor cats live longer mainly because they avoid cars, poisons, predators and diseases picked up from other cats.
The oldest cats on record have lived into their late thirties, but anything past 20 is exceptional.
Senior cats often sleep more, play less and lose some muscle along the back and hind legs.
Older cats commonly groom themselves less thoroughly, so their coat may look duller or matted and need brushing.
Many elderly cats develop arthritis, which usually shows as hesitation before jumping rather than an obvious limp.
A cat that suddenly stops jumping onto its favourite high spots is often sore in its joints, not just getting lazy.
Older cats' claws often grow thicker and more brittle, and need trimming more often to stop them curling into the pad.
Vets suggest a check-up at least once a year for adult cats and twice a year for seniors.
Many senior cats develop a bluish haze in the lens from normal ageing (lenticular sclerosis) that barely affects sight.
Weight loss in an older cat is a common early sign of illness such as kidney disease, hyperthyroidism or diabetes.
Some elderly cats show signs of dementia, such as yowling at night or seeming lost in familiar rooms.
Hearing loss is common in very old cats, which can make them startle more easily and meow more loudly.
Elderly cats with stiff joints find high-sided litter trays harder to climb into.
Senior cat food is often lower in calories but not lower in protein, because older cats lose muscle easily.
Cats with white or pale ears and noses can get sunburn, which over the years can turn into skin cancer on the ear tips.
Cats are obligate carnivores: they are built to get key nutrients from meat and cannot thrive on a plant-only diet.
Taurine is an amino acid cats cannot make enough of themselves, so it has to come from the meat in their diet.
Without enough taurine, a cat can go blind from damage to the retina and develop a weak, enlarged heart.
Dogs can make their own taurine, which is one reason dog food is not a safe long-term diet for a cat.
Cats need a much higher share of protein in their food than dogs or people do.
Cats cannot turn plant beta-carotene into vitamin A, so they need it ready-made from animal foods such as liver.
Too much liver can give a cat vitamin A poisoning, which causes painful bony growths along the neck and spine.
Cats cannot make arachidonic acid, a fatty acid found in animal fat, so it has to be in their food.
Raw fish contains an enzyme that destroys vitamin B1, so a cat fed mostly raw fish can develop a serious deficiency.
A diet of tinned tuna meant for people lacks nutrients a cat needs and can build up mercury over time.
Most adult cats are lactose intolerant: after weaning they make much less lactase, the enzyme that digests milk sugar.
Cow's milk gives many cats diarrhoea, so the traditional saucer of milk often does more harm than good.
Cat milk sold in pet shops has had most of the lactose removed, which is why cats can digest it.
Lilies are deadly to cats: even licking pollen off the fur or drinking the vase water can cause kidney failure.
True lilies and day lilies attack the kidneys. Peace and calla lilies only irritate the mouth and are far less harmful.
Onions, garlic, leeks and chives damage a cat's red blood cells and can cause anaemia, whether raw, cooked or powdered.
Chocolate contains theobromine, which cats cannot break down well. Dark and baking chocolate are the most dangerous.
Grapes and raisins have caused kidney failure in dogs and are treated as unsafe for cats as well.
Xylitol, a sweetener in sugar-free gum, is toxic to dogs and is treated as unsafe for cats as a precaution.
Paracetamol (acetaminophen), a common human painkiller, is extremely poisonous to cats. A single tablet can kill.
Cats are so sensitive to paracetamol because they lack a liver enzyme that most mammals use to break it down.
Human painkillers such as ibuprofen and aspirin are also toxic to cats, which cannot process them safely.
Antifreeze containing ethylene glycol is deadly to cats. As little as a teaspoon can cause fatal kidney failure.
Many essential oils, including tea tree, eucalyptus, peppermint and citrus oils, are toxic to cats, even when diffused.
Raw bread dough is dangerous to cats: the yeast keeps rising in the warm stomach and produces alcohol as it ferments.
Alcohol in any form is poisonous to cats, and their small size means a tiny amount can do serious harm.
Caffeine, in coffee, tea and energy drinks, is toxic to cats in much the same way chocolate is.
Many common houseplants are toxic to cats, including sago palm, dieffenbachia, pothos, cyclamen and azalea.
Tulip and daffodil bulbs are poisonous to cats. Poinsettia, by contrast, is only mildly irritating despite its bad name.
Lily of the valley is also poisonous to cats, but it affects the heart rather than the kidneys.
Permethrin, found in many dog flea treatments, is highly toxic to cats, so dog flea products are never safe on a cat.
Disinfectants containing phenol are especially dangerous to cats, which lick any residue off their paws and fur.
Rodent poisons can kill a cat either directly or when the cat eats a poisoned mouse.
Cooked bones can splinter and lodge in a cat's throat or gut, which is why they are left out of cat diets.
Raw eggs and raw meat can carry salmonella and other bacteria that make cats, and the people around them, ill.
Cats descend from desert animals and have a weak thirst drive, so they tend to drink little water.
A cat's kidneys are very good at concentrating urine, which is how cats can survive on so little water.
Wet cat food is about 75 to 80 percent water, so cats eating it get much of their daily water from their meals.
Cats fed only dry food drink more from the bowl than cats on wet food, but still take in less water overall.
Many cats prefer running water, which is why a dripping tap or a pet fountain can tempt a reluctant drinker.
Many cats dislike drinking right beside their food, and drink more when the water bowl sits in a separate spot.
Signs of dehydration in a cat include sticky gums and skin that is slow to spring back when gently lifted.
A typical 4 kilogram (9 pound) indoor cat needs only around 200 to 250 calories a day, about one small bagel's worth.
Cats are built to eat many small meals, often ten or more a day, rather than one or two big ones.
Treats should make up no more than about 10 percent of a cat's daily calories.
Obesity is the most common nutritional problem in pet cats. Surveys suggest around half of pet cats are overweight.
A cat is classed as obese when it is about 20 percent or more above its ideal weight.
An extra kilogram on a 4 kilogram cat is, in proportion, like an extra 20 kilograms on an average adult.
A healthy cat has a visible waist from above and ribs that can be felt under a thin layer of fat but not seen.
Overweight cats are far more likely to develop diabetes, arthritis and urinary problems, and tend to die younger.
A cat that stops eating or slims too fast can develop hepatic lipidosis, a dangerous build-up of fat in the liver.
Safe weight loss for a cat is slow, no more than about 1 to 2 percent of body weight a week.
Leaving dry food out all day is a common cause of weight gain in indoor cats, which have little chance to burn it off.
Puzzle feeders make a cat work for its food, which helps with both weight control and boredom.
Cat grass (usually wheat or oat grass) is safe for cats to nibble and is sold for indoor cats that like greenery.
Dental disease is one of the most common health problems in cats. Most cats over three show some sign of gum disease.
Cats rarely get cavities the way people do. Their main dental problems are tartar, gum disease and tooth resorption.
Tooth resorption, where the body slowly dissolves a tooth from the inside, is common in adult cats and is painful.
Bad breath in a cat is usually a sign of dental disease rather than of something it ate.
Cats with sore mouths often keep eating but swallow dry food whole or drop it, so dental pain is easy to miss.
Many cats accept tooth brushing with a cat toothpaste if it is introduced slowly. Human toothpaste is not safe for them.
Cats get dental cleaning under general anaesthetic, because tartar below the gumline cannot be removed otherwise.
Hairballs start with grooming: the tiny hooks on a cat's tongue pull out loose fur, which the cat then swallows.
Most swallowed fur passes through a cat harmlessly. A hairball is the fur that stays in the stomach and comes back up.
A hairball is usually sausage-shaped, not round, because it is squeezed through the narrow food pipe on its way out.
Long-haired cats, and any cat shedding heavily in spring, produce more hairballs. Regular brushing cuts them down.
Hairballs more than about once a month can signal over-grooming or a digestive problem rather than normal shedding.
Hairball remedies lubricate the gut or add fibre, so that swallowed fur passes through instead of coming back up.
A cat's coughing fits are often mistaken for attempts to bring up a hairball, but they can be a sign of feline asthma.
A single flea can lay around 50 eggs a day, and the eggs drop off the cat into carpets and bedding.
Only about 5 percent of a flea problem is adult fleas on the cat. The rest is eggs, larvae and pupae around the home.
Indoor cats can still get fleas, which arrive on clothing, shoes or visiting pets.
Fleas can pass tapeworms to cats: a cat swallows an infected flea while grooming and the worm grows in its gut.
Cats allergic to flea bites can get sore, scabby skin from just one or two bites, especially along the back and neck.
A heavy flea infestation can cause anaemia in a kitten, because the fleas drink so much of its blood.
Cat scratch disease is caused by a bacterium that cats pick up from fleas, and kittens are the most likely to carry it.
Cats that hunt need worming more often than indoor cats, because mice and birds carry worm larvae.
Ear mites are a common cause of itchy ears in kittens and leave a dark, crumbly discharge much like coffee grounds.
Ringworm in cats is a fungus, not a worm. It shows as circular bald patches and can spread to people.
Core cat vaccines cover panleukopenia (a deadly virus) and the two main causes of cat flu, herpesvirus and calicivirus.
Feline panleukopenia is caused by a parvovirus closely related to the one that causes parvo in dogs.
The panleukopenia virus can survive in the environment for up to a year, so even indoor cats are vaccinated against it.
Cat flu viruses can leave a cat as a lifelong carrier that flares up under stress, much like cold sores in people.
After the kitten course, most cats have a booster at one year old, then every one to three years depending on the jab.
Rabies vaccination is required by law for cats in many places, including much of the United States.
FIV, feline immunodeficiency virus, is a cat-only relative of HIV and cannot infect people.
FIV spreads mainly through deep bite wounds, so unneutered outdoor toms that fight are the most often infected.
Many FIV-positive cats live long, normal lives, especially when kept indoors and away from other infections.
Feline leukaemia virus (FeLV) spreads through close contact like grooming and shared bowls, and from mother to kitten.
A vaccine exists for feline leukaemia virus, and it is often recommended for kittens and for cats that go outdoors.
Both FIV and FeLV can be detected with a quick blood test at the vet.
Chronic kidney disease is very common in old cats, and the risk climbs steeply after about the age of ten.
The earliest signs of kidney disease in cats are usually drinking more and peeing more, followed by weight loss.
Kidney disease in cats often causes no outward signs until about two thirds of kidney function has already been lost.
Kidney disease in cats cannot be cured, but special diets and fluids often give affected cats good extra years.
Cats with kidney disease are often fed a diet lower in phosphorus, which slows the progress of the disease.
Cats get a form of diabetes much like type 2 in people, and overweight middle-aged male cats are at the highest risk.
Most diabetic cats are treated with insulin injections at home twice a day, which most cats tolerate well.
With insulin and a low-carbohydrate diet, some diabetic cats go into remission and no longer need injections.
A diabetic cat that walks on its hocks (ankles) instead of its toes may have nerve damage from high blood sugar.
Hyperthyroidism, an overactive thyroid gland, is the most common hormone disorder in older cats.
A hyperthyroid cat typically eats ravenously yet loses weight, and may be restless, vocal and have a racing heart.
Hyperthyroidism in cats can often be cured outright with a single treatment of radioactive iodine.
Hyperthyroidism was almost unknown in cats before the late 1970s, and why it became so common is not fully understood.
High blood pressure is common in older cats, often alongside kidney disease or hyperthyroidism, and can cause blindness.
Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, a thickening of the heart muscle, is the most common heart disease in cats.
A urinary blockage is an emergency in male cats, whose narrow urethra can be plugged by crystals or mucus within hours.
Cat bites to people get infected far more often than dog bites, as the needle-like teeth push bacteria deep into tissue.
A microchip is about the size of a grain of rice and is injected under the skin between the shoulder blades.
A microchip has no battery. A scanner powers it for a moment and reads a unique number linked to the owner's details.
Microchipping has been compulsory for pet cats in England since June 2024.
Some cat flaps and feeders read a cat's microchip and open only for that cat.
Indoor cats need outlets for hunting behaviour: toys that move, scratching posts, high perches and places to hide.
Cats use vertical space, so shelves and tall perches effectively enlarge a cat's territory in a small home.
Several short play sessions a day suit cats better than one long one, matching the rhythm of hunting in short bursts.
A study of shelter cats found that having a cardboard box to hide in helped them settle into a new place faster.
The usual rule for litter trays is one per cat plus one extra, placed in different quiet spots around the home.
Most cats prefer unscented, fine-grained clumping litter and a tray big enough to turn around in comfortably.
A dirty or badly placed litter tray is one of the most common reasons a cat starts toileting elsewhere in the home.
Short-haired cats need brushing about once a week. Long-haired cats need combing daily to stop painful mats forming.
Mats in a long coat pull on the skin and can trap moisture and dirt, leading to sores and infection underneath.
Cats spend a large share of their waking hours grooming, often estimated at 30 to 50 percent.
Cats shed most heavily in spring and autumn as the coat changes. Indoor cats shed more evenly all year round.
Most cats never need a bath: their own grooming keeps them clean, and bathing strips protective oils from the coat.
Hairless cats are the exception: with no fur to absorb skin oils, they need regular baths or wipes.
A cat that gets something sticky, oily or toxic on its coat does need washing, with a cat shampoo and lukewarm water.
A cat's claws grow continuously, and the old outer layer is shed as a hollow sheath, often found near scratching posts.
Only the clear tip of a claw is cut when trimming. The pink area inside (the quick) holds blood vessels and nerves.
Cats have a fifth claw on each front paw, the dewclaw, which does not touch the ground and can overgrow if not trimmed.
Cats hide pain well: a cat in pain often simply withdraws, hides, grooms less and sits hunched with half-closed eyes.
The Feline Grimace Scale lets vets score a cat's pain from its ears, eyes, muzzle, whiskers and head position.
Going off food, hiding and a scruffy, unkempt coat are among the earliest signs that a cat is unwell.
A cat's normal body temperature is about 38 to 39 C (100.5 to 102.5 F), a degree or two warmer than a person's.
A resting cat's heart beats around 140 to 220 times a minute, two to three times faster than an adult human's.
A cat takes about 20 to 30 breaths a minute at rest, compared with 12 to 20 for an adult person.
The first cloned cat, named CC (short for Copy Cat), was born in Texas in December 2001.
CC the cloned cat did not look exactly like her genetic donor, because a cat's coat pattern is partly set in the womb.
CC the cloned cat lived a normal life, had kittens of her own and died in 2020 at the age of 18.
The first cat genome to be sequenced belonged to an Abyssinian named Cinnamon, and it was published in 2007.
Cat genome research helps human medicine too, because cats get many of the same inherited diseases as people.
There are thought to be several hundred million pet cats in the world.
In several countries, including Germany and Russia, pet cats outnumber pet dogs.
Most cat allergies are to a protein called Fel d 1, which is made in the cat's saliva and skin glands, not in the fur.
Cats spread Fel d 1 onto their fur when they groom, and the dried flakes float in the air and settle on furniture.
Male cats produce more Fel d 1 than females, and neutered males produce less than unneutered ones.
No cat is truly hypoallergenic, though some individual cats produce much less Fel d 1 than others.
A cat food now exists that binds Fel d 1 in the cat's saliva using egg antibodies, reducing the allergen on its fur.
Cats are not truly nocturnal: they are crepuscular, most active around dawn and dusk when their prey is about.
A cat's tail is part of its spine, so a hard pull or a slammed door can damage the nerves that control the bladder.
Tail-pull injuries are common in cats hit by cars, when the tail is caught under a wheel as the cat runs.
Cats can live well without a tail after an amputation for injury, though they take a while to adjust their balance.
Declawing is not a nail trim: it amputates the last bone of each toe, the one the claw grows from.
Declawing is illegal in the UK, much of Europe, Australia and New Zealand, and in several US states and cities.
New York became the first US state to ban declawing, in 2019.
Studies have linked declawing to higher rates of biting and litter tray avoidance in the cats that had it done.
Cats pass the toxoplasmosis parasite in their faeces, but usually only for a couple of weeks after first catching it.
Toxoplasma in cat faeces takes a day or more to become infectious, so a litter tray scooped daily carries little risk.
People are more likely to catch toxoplasmosis from undercooked meat or unwashed vegetables than from their own cat.
Pregnant women are advised to let someone else clean the litter tray, or to wear gloves and wash their hands afterwards.
Most cats with toxoplasmosis show no signs at all, and once infected they rarely shed the parasite again.
