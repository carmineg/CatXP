The tabby is a coat pattern, not a breed, and it is the most common coat pattern among domestic cats.
There are four basic tabby patterns: mackerel, classic, spotted and ticked.
A mackerel tabby has narrow stripes running down its sides like fish bones, which is how the pattern got its name.
The classic tabby, also called blotched, has wide swirls on its sides that often form a bullseye shape.
A spotted tabby has spots along its sides instead of stripes. The spots are thought to be broken-up stripes.
A ticked tabby has bands of colour on each hair but few or no stripes on its body, giving a salt-and-pepper look.
The ticked pattern is dominant over the other tabby patterns, and mackerel is dominant over classic.
Nearly every tabby has an M-shaped mark on its forehead, formed by the stripes above the eyes.
One legend says the M on a tabby's forehead was left by Mary, who stroked a cat that warmed the infant Jesus.
Another legend credits the tabby's M to the Prophet Muhammad, who was said to be very fond of his cat Muezza.
A third legend says the tabby's M stands for Mau, the ancient Egyptian word for cat.
The word tabby comes from a striped silk cloth once made in the Attabiyah district of Baghdad.
The tabby pattern is the wild type, meaning it is the natural coat of the cat's ancestor, the African wildcat.
Every cat is a tabby underneath: even a solid black cat carries a tabby pattern that is simply hidden.
The agouti gene lets a tabby's pattern show by giving each hair in the pale areas bands of light and dark colour.
A cat with two copies of the non-agouti gene looks solid because the pale bands on its hairs fill in with dark pigment.
A deletion of just two DNA letters in the agouti gene is what turns a striped cat into a solid-coloured one.
Faint tabby stripes, called ghost markings, can often be seen on a solid black cat in bright sunlight.
The classic tabby pattern is recessive: a cat needs two copies of the blotched gene to show the swirls.
The gene behind the classic tabby swirls was identified in 2012 and is called Taqpep.
Orange cats are always tabbies: the orange gene stops the solid-colour gene from hiding the stripes.
Tabby stripe patterns vary from cat to cat, and no two tabbies are marked exactly alike.
A so-called brown tabby is genetically a black cat. The warm brown comes from the pale bands on its agouti hairs.
A tortoiseshell cat has a coat of mixed black and orange patches, usually with little or no white.
A calico cat is a tortoiseshell with white: patches of black and orange on a white background.
In Britain a calico is usually called a tortoiseshell-and-white, and in Japan it is a mi-ke, meaning three fur.
The gene for orange fur sits on the X chromosome, so it is inherited differently by males and females.
Female cats have two X chromosomes, so one can carry orange and the other black, which produces tortoiseshell patches.
Early in a female embryo, each cell switches off one of its two X chromosomes at random: this is X-inactivation.
X-inactivation is why a tortoiseshell's orange and black patches are scattered randomly across her coat.
A male cat has only one X chromosome, so he is normally either orange or not orange, but not both.
Roughly 1 in 3,000 tortoiseshell or calico cats is male.
Most male tortoiseshell cats have an extra X chromosome (XXY), similar to Klinefelter syndrome in humans.
Male calico cats with XXY chromosomes are almost always sterile.
A male tortoiseshell can also arise as a chimera, when two embryos fuse into one cat early in development.
Both parents of a tortoiseshell kitten must between them carry the orange and the non-orange versions of the gene.
A dilute tortoiseshell, with patches of blue-grey and cream instead of black and orange, is called a blue-cream.
Torbie is the nickname for a tortoiseshell cat whose coloured patches also show tabby stripes.
A calico whose patches show tabby stripes is sometimes called a caliby.
Tortoiseshell cats often have mottled noses and paw pads, with pink and dark areas matching the patches on their fur.
White spotting tends to give a calico larger, more sharply defined patches than a tortoiseshell with no white has.
The first cloned cat, CC, short for CopyCat, was born in December 2001 at a university in Texas.
CC did not match her calico donor: X-inactivation is random, so the clone came out grey tabby and white with no orange.
The gene that makes cats orange, ARHGAP36, was pinned down by two research teams whose findings were published in 2025.
Roughly 80 percent of orange cats are male, because a male needs only one copy of the X-linked orange gene.
A female orange cat needs an orange gene from both parents, so her father must be orange.
A male kitten's only X chromosome comes from his mother, so an orange male's mother is always orange or tortoiseshell.
In the show world orange cats are called red, while everyday names include ginger and marmalade.
Orange cats often develop small dark freckles, called lentigo, on their lips, nose, gums and eyelids.
Orange fur is made of phaeomelanin, the same pigment that gives people red hair.
A cat with the dominant white gene is white all over, no matter what colour genes it carries underneath.
The dominant white gene works by stopping pigment cells from reaching the skin while the kitten develops in the womb.
A white cat with the dominant white gene may be a hidden tortoiseshell, orange or tabby, and can pass those colours on.
Two white cats can have coloured kittens if each parent carries only one copy of the dominant white gene.
Only about 5 percent of cats are white, and most white cats can hear normally.
White cats with blue eyes are far more likely to be deaf than white cats with green, gold or copper eyes.
Around 65 to 85 percent of white cats with two blue eyes are deaf.
Around 17 to 22 percent of white cats with eyes of a colour other than blue are deaf.
A white cat with one blue eye and one of another colour is often deaf only on the side with the blue eye.
White fur, blue eyes and deafness are linked because pigment cells are needed to keep the inner ear working.
A deaf white kitten's hearing organ is not missing at birth. It degenerates during the first few weeks of life.
Many white kittens are born with a small smudge of colour on the head that fades away as they grow.
White cats and albino cats are different: a true albino has no pigment at all and very pale pinkish-blue eyes.
True albino cats are very rare. They carry two copies of a recessive form of the gene behind the Siamese points.
The Siamese colourpoint pattern is a form of partial albinism: pigment is only made in the cooler parts of the body.
The colourpoint gene is a heat-sensitive version of tyrosinase, the enzyme that makes the pigment melanin.
Siamese kittens are born almost pure white because the womb is warm all over, so no pigment forms before birth.
A Siamese cat's points, the ears, face, paws and tail, darken within a few weeks of birth as those parts cool down.
Colourpoint cats living in cold places tend to develop darker bodies than those living in warm homes.
If a patch of a Siamese cat's fur is shaved, the fur can grow back darker because the bare skin was cooler.
Colourpoint cats tend to darken with age, so an old Siamese often has far less contrast than a young one.
A cat needs two copies of the colourpoint gene to have Siamese-style points. A cat with one copy looks normal.
A seal point Siamese is genetically a black cat whose colour shows only on the cooler points.
The four traditional Siamese point colours are seal, blue, chocolate and lilac.
A lynx point cat is a colourpoint whose points show tabby stripes.
Cats with the colourpoint gene always have blue eyes, because the same gene reduces pigment in the iris.
Many Siamese cats have a slight squint, linked to the colourpoint gene affecting how the nerves from the eyes are wired.
The Burmese sepia gene is a milder version of the same mutation: the body is only slightly paler than the points.
A cat with one Siamese gene and one Burmese gene has a coat halfway between the two, a pattern called mink.
Tuxedo cats are black with white on the chest, paws and belly, a look caused by the white spotting gene.
The white spotting gene, also called piebald, is different from the dominant white gene that makes a cat entirely white.
Dominant white and white spotting in cats are both caused by different changes in a single gene called KIT.
White spots usually appear on the parts of the body that pigment cells reach last: the belly, chest, paws and chin.
A locket is a small spot of white on the chest of an otherwise solid-coloured cat.
White mittens, white toes and a white chin are the lowest grades of white spotting.
Breeders grade white spotting on a scale of 1 to 10, from a tiny locket to an almost entirely white cat.
A cat that is mostly white with colour only on the head and tail is said to have the van pattern.
A harlequin cat is mostly white with several large patches of colour on the body.
White spotting is dominant: one copy is enough to produce white patches, though the amount of white varies a lot.
A smoke cat looks solid-coloured at rest, but its hairs are white at the root, so the coat flashes pale when it moves.
Smoke, shaded and chinchilla coats come from the inhibitor gene, which blocks pigment on the lower part of each hair.
The inhibitor gene is dominant, so one copy is enough to make a silver tabby, a smoke or a shaded cat.
A chinchilla cat, also called shell or tipped, has pigment only on the very tip of each hair, giving a sparkling look.
In show terms a chinchilla has colour on about the top eighth of each hair, and a shaded cat on about the top third.
A silver tabby is a tabby whose pale background hairs have been turned white by the inhibitor gene.
The recessive dilute gene lightens colour: black becomes blue, chocolate becomes lilac and orange becomes cream.
A blue cat is really grey: in cat fancy terms, blue is the name for the dilute form of black.
Old cat books call blue cats Maltese, a name still heard in some places.
Dilution works by clumping the pigment granules in each hair unevenly, so the colour looks paler.
The gene behind dilute colour is melanophilin (MLPH), and a cat needs two copies of the dilute version to be blue.
Two black cats can have blue kittens if both parents carry a hidden copy of the recessive dilute gene.
Lilac, also called lavender, is the dilute form of chocolate, and fawn is the dilute form of cinnamon.
Cream is the dilute form of orange, so a cream cat carries the orange gene plus two copies of the dilute gene.
Chocolate and cinnamon fur come from two different recessive versions of the gene TYRP1, which alters black pigment.
The sorrel colour of the Abyssinian is genetically cinnamon, a light reddish-brown form of black pigment.
Amber, a rare colour seen in Norwegian Forest Cats, comes from a change in the gene MC1R: black kittens turn golden.
Genetically, every cat colour is a variation on black or orange, plus white where pigment is missing.
There is no gene for white pigment: white fur is simply hair with no pigment in it.
A cat's coat colours come from two pigments: eumelanin for black and brown, and phaeomelanin for red and yellow.
A cat's nose and paw pad colour usually follows its coat genes: black cats have black pads and orange cats pink ones.
Eye colour comes from melanin in the iris: a lot gives copper or gold, less gives green, and almost none gives blue.
All kittens are born with blue eyes, because the iris has not yet produced its pigment.
A kitten's adult eye colour starts to show at around six or seven weeks and is usually settled by three to four months.
Blue eyes in cats contain no blue pigment: they look blue because light scatters in an iris with little or no melanin.
Copper is the darkest eye colour that cats normally have.
Odd-eyed cats have one blue eye and one green, yellow or copper eye, a condition called heterochromia.
Odd eyes are most common in white cats and cats with lots of white, because those genes keep pigment out of one eye.
Odd-eyed cats start life with two blue eyes. One eye then gains colour over a few months while the other stays blue.
An odd-eyed cat sees equally well with both eyes, as the difference in colour does not affect its vision.
Some cats have two colours within a single iris, a rarer form of heterochromia called sectoral or dichroic.
Eye colour is mostly independent of coat colour, with exceptions such as white cats and colourpoints.
Polydactyly means having extra toes. A normal cat has 18, five on each front paw and four on each back paw.
Polydactyly is usually caused by a dominant gene, so a polydactyl parent often passes extra toes to many of its kittens.
Polydactyl cats are especially common along the east coast of North America and in south-west England and Wales.
Polydactyly is thought to have spread with ships, since sailors valued the big-pawed cats as mousers and lucky charms.
The Ernest Hemingway Home in Key West, Florida, keeps around 60 cats, about half of them polydactyl.
Hemingway's first polydactyl cat, Snow White, was reportedly given to him by a ship's captain.
Polydactyl cats are nicknamed Hemingway cats, mitten cats and thumb cats.
The most toes on a cat on record is 28: Jake, a ginger tabby from Ontario, Canada, had seven toes on each paw.
Polydactyly usually affects the front paws, and extra toes on the back paws alone are rare.
The change behind the Hemingway cats' extra toes lies in a control region of a gene called Sonic hedgehog.
Many early Maine Coons were polydactyl, but extra toes were later barred from the show ring for the breed.
The Manx's missing tail comes from a dominant gene that shortens the spine. Kittens with two copies die before birth.
Because the Manx gene is lethal in a double dose, every tailless Manx has one tailless gene and one normal gene.
Manx cats come in rumpy (no tail), rumpy riser (a small bump), stumpy (a short tail) and longy (a near-full tail).
The Manx mutation lies in a gene called T, or brachyury, which guides how the spine forms in the embryo.
A folk tale says the Manx lost its tail when Noah shut the Ark door on it. In reality a gene mutation is responsible.
The Japanese Bobtail's pom-pom tail comes from a recessive gene, unlike the dominant Manx gene, and is harmless.
The gene behind the Japanese Bobtail's kinked tail, HES7, was identified in 2016. It controls how vertebrae form.
No two Japanese Bobtail tails are alike: the kinks and curves differ from cat to cat.
Kinked and bobbed tails are common among street cats in Japan and much of Southeast Asia.
Long hair in cats is recessive: a longhaired kitten needs a long-hair gene from both parents.
Two shorthaired cats can produce a longhaired kitten if both carry the hidden long-hair gene.
A longhaired cat has two copies of the long-hair gene, so every one of its kittens carries at least one copy.
Long fur in cats comes from changes in the gene FGF5, which also controls hair length in dogs and other mammals.
At least four different mutations in FGF5 cause long hair in cat breeds, and all of them have the same effect.
FGF5 normally tells a hair to stop growing. When the gene is broken, each hair keeps growing longer before it is shed.
The Sphynx's hairlessness comes from a recessive change in the gene KRT71, which makes a protein in the hair shaft.
A different change in the same KRT71 gene gives the Devon Rex its curly coat, and a third curls the Selkirk Rex.
Sphynx cats are not truly hairless: they are covered in a fine down that feels like warm suede or chamois leather.
The Sphynx breed traces back to a hairless kitten called Prune, born in Toronto in 1966.
The Donskoy, a hairless cat from Russia, owes its bare skin to a dominant gene, unlike the recessive gene of the Sphynx.
The Donskoy breed began with a kitten called Varvara, found in Rostov-on-Don in 1987.
Hairless cats still have colour genes: their skin shows the pattern the fur would have had, including tabby stripes.
Sphynx cats often have few whiskers, or whiskers that are short and broken.
The Lykoi, or werewolf cat, has a sparse, patchy coat caused by recessive changes in the gene HR.
Rex cats are named after Rex rabbits, which have a similar plush, wavy coat.
The Cornish Rex has no guard hairs at all, only the soft undercoat hairs, which lie in tight waves.
The Cornish Rex gene is recessive and first appeared in a kitten called Kallibunker, born in Cornwall in 1950.
The Cornish Rex curl comes from a change in the gene LPAR6, identified in 2013.
The Devon Rex arose from a separate recessive mutation found in Devon in 1960, a different gene from the Cornish Rex.
Devon Rex whiskers are short and brittle and often break off.
The Selkirk Rex's curl is dominant, so a kitten needs only one curly parent to inherit the wavy coat.
The LaPerm's curls come from a dominant gene. The first LaPerm was born bald in Oregon in 1982 and grew curls later.
The American Wirehair's springy coat comes from a dominant mutation found in a litter in upstate New York in 1966.
A fever coat happens when a pregnant cat has a fever or severe stress, so her kittens are born with pale, silvery fur.
Fever coat kittens grow out of it: their true colour comes in as the pale fur sheds over the first months.
A fever coat is not inherited. It is thought to be caused by heat disturbing pigment while the kittens are in the womb.
Black cats can rust: hours in the sun can turn a black coat reddish brown as sunlight breaks down the pigment.
Black fur that looks rusty can also mean a diet short of the amino acid tyrosine, which cats use to make dark pigment.
Kittens in one litter can look nothing alike because each one gets a random half of each parent's genes.
A female cat can mate with several males during one heat, so a single litter can have more than one father.
The domestic cat has 38 chromosomes, arranged in 19 pairs, while humans have 46 and dogs have 78.
The cat genome was first sequenced in 2007 from an Abyssinian cat named Cinnamon.
The cat genome contains roughly 20,000 protein-coding genes, a similar number to the human genome.
In 2004 a Texas woman paid 50,000 dollars for Little Nicky, the first commercially cloned pet cat.
A cat's coat has three kinds of hair: long guard hairs, mid-length awn hairs and the soft, short down hairs beneath.
A cat's fur is dense, with roughly 10,000 to 20,000 hairs per square centimetre, thicker on the belly than on the back.
The oldest cat on record, Creme Puff of Austin, Texas, lived 38 years and 3 days, from 1967 to 2005.
Creme Puff's owner, Jake Perry, also owned Granpa Rex Allen, who held the oldest cat record before her at 34 years.
Jake Perry reportedly fed his record-breaking cats a breakfast of eggs, turkey bacon, broccoli and coffee with cream.
Earlier Guinness books listed a tabby called Puss from Devon, England, who was said to have died in 1939 aged 36.
Flossie, a British tortoiseshell-and-white born in December 1995, was named the oldest living cat in 2022 at nearly 27.
Scooter, a Siamese from Mansfield, Texas, was recognised as the oldest living cat in 2016 at the age of 30.
Corduroy, a cat from Oregon born in 1989, was recognised as the oldest living cat in 2015 at the age of 26.
Tiffany Two, a tortoiseshell from San Diego, held the oldest living cat title before she died in 2015 aged 27.
Poppy, a cat from Bournemouth, England, was named the oldest living cat in 2014 at 24, shortly before she died.
Rubble, a Maine Coon from Exeter, England, was reported to be 31 in 2019, but his age was never officially verified.
The longest domestic cat on record was Stewie, a Maine Coon from Reno, Nevada, measuring 123 cm (48.5 in) nose to tail.
Stewie, whose full name was Mymains Stewart Gilligan, was a certified therapy animal who visited a local senior centre.
Stewie set his record in 2010 and died of cancer in 2013.
Before Stewie, the longest cat record belonged to Leo, a Maine Coon from Chicago measured at 121.9 cm (48 in) in 2002.
Every holder of the longest domestic cat record so far has been a Maine Coon.
Barivel, a Maine Coon from Vigevano, Italy, was named the longest domestic cat living in 2018 at 120 cm (3 ft 11.2 in).
Barivel's name means clown or joker in a local Italian dialect, though his owners describe him as shy and quiet.
Ludo, a Maine Coon from Wakefield, England, held the longest living cat record in 2015 at 118.33 cm (3 ft 10.59 in).
The tallest domestic cat on record was Arcturus, a Savannah from Michigan, who stood 48.4 cm (19.05 in) at the shoulder.
Savannah cats are tall because they descend from the serval, a long-legged African wild cat.
Cygnus Regulus Powers, a silver Maine Coon, had the longest tail on a domestic cat on record at 44.66 cm (17.58 in).
Arcturus and Cygnus lived in the same home in Farmington Hills, Michigan, and both died in a house fire in 2017.
The same family later owned Fenrir, a Savannah recognised in 2021 as the tallest living cat at 47.83 cm (18.83 in).
Altair, a silver Maine Coon from Michigan, was recognised in 2021 for the longest tail on a living cat, 40.7 cm.
Trouble, a Savannah from Sacramento, California, held the tallest cat record in 2011 at 48.3 cm (19 in).
Stewie also held a record for the longest tail on a domestic cat, 41.5 cm (16.34 in), before Cygnus beat it.
The heaviest cat on record, Himmy, a tabby from Cairns, Australia, weighed 21.3 kg (46 lb 15 oz) when he died in 1986.
Himmy's waist measured 84 cm (33 in), and his owner had to carry him around in a wheelbarrow.
Guinness World Records retired its heaviest pet categories in the late 1990s so owners would not overfeed pets to win.
An 18 kg cat called Meow drew headlines in 2012, but Guinness no longer lists heaviest cats, so no record was set.
The smallest cat on record was Tinker Toy, a Himalayan-Persian from Illinois, just 7 cm (2.75 in) tall fully grown.
Tinker Toy measured 19 cm (7.5 in) long. He was the runt of a litter of six and lived from 1990 to 1997.
Lilieput, a Munchkin from Napa, California, was recognised in 2013 as the shortest living cat at 13.34 cm (5.25 in).
Fizz Girl, a Munchkin from San Diego, held the shortest living cat record in 2010 at 15.24 cm (6 in) tall.
The loudest purr by a domestic cat on record is 67.8 decibels, by Merlin, a rescue cat from Torquay, England, in 2015.
Merlin's purr was about as loud as a dishwasher, while a typical cat purrs at around 25 decibels.
Before Merlin, the loudest purr on record was 67.68 decibels, by Smokey from Northamptonshire, England, in 2011.
In 2023 Guinness recognised Bella, from Huntingdon, England, for the loudest purr by a living cat at 54.59 decibels.
The most prolific cat on record was Dusty, a tabby from Bonham, Texas, who produced 420 kittens in her lifetime.
Dusty's last litter, a single kitten, was born in June 1952.
The largest litter on record was 19 kittens, born on 7 August 1970 to a Burmese-Siamese cat in Oxfordshire, England.
Of the record litter of 19 kittens born in 1970, four were stillborn and 15 survived.
A typical cat litter has three to five kittens, so a litter of 19 is about four times the usual size.
The oldest cat on record to give birth was Kitty, from Staffordshire, England, who had two kittens in 1987 aged 30.
The longest cat whiskers on record measured 19 cm (7.5 in) and belonged to Missi, a Maine Coon from Finland, in 2005.
Colonel Meow, a Himalayan-Persian cross from Los Angeles, held the longest fur record in 2013 with hairs of 22.87 cm.
The longest fur on a cat on record is 25.68 cm (10.11 in), measured on Sophie Smith of Oceanside, California, in 2013.
Alley, a rescue cat from Austin, Texas, set a record for the longest jump by a cat in 2013 at 182.88 cm (6 ft).
Waffle the Warrior Cat, from Big Sur, California, raised the longest jump record to 213.36 cm (7 ft) in 2018.
The most tricks by a cat in one minute on record is 26, by Alexis and her trainer Anika Moritz in Austria in 2020.
Didga, a cat from Tweed Heads, Australia, set a record of 24 tricks in one minute in 2016, including a skateboard jump.
The fastest domestic cat breed on record is the Egyptian Mau, clocked at around 48 km/h (30 mph).
A healthy domestic cat can sprint at around 48 km/h (30 mph), faster than Usain Bolt's top speed of about 45 km/h.
Sugar, a cat from Boston, survived a fall of 19 floors in 2012, landing on a patch of mulch with only minor injuries.
A 1987 study of 132 cats that fell from New York high-rises found 90 percent survived. It named high-rise syndrome.
The 1987 study found cats that fell from seven or more storeys had fewer injuries than those that fell from two to six.
A falling cat reaches its top speed, about 100 km/h (60 mph), after roughly five storeys, then relaxes and spreads out.
Blackie, a British cat, inherited 7 million pounds when his owner Ben Rea died in 1988, the wealthiest cat on record.
Ben Rea left his fortune to Blackie, the last of his 15 cats, and to three cat charities, leaving out his family.
Karl Lagerfeld said his Birman cat Choupette would be one of his heirs. She reportedly earned 3 million euros in 2014.
A former stray named Tommaso in Rome was reportedly left an estate worth around 10 million euros by his owner in 2011.
Nala, a Siamese-tabby cross adopted from a Los Angeles shelter, set the record for most Instagram followers by a cat.
Nala passed 4.3 million Instagram followers when her record was confirmed in 2020.
The greatest mouser on record was Towser, a tortoiseshell who caught an estimated 28,899 mice at a Scottish distillery.
Towser's tally was worked out by observers who counted her catches over several days and scaled up over her 24 years.
Towser averaged roughly three mice a day, and a bronze statue of her stands at the Glenturret distillery in Scotland.
Holly, a cat from West Palm Beach, Florida, walked about 300 km (190 miles) home after getting lost at Daytona Beach.
Holly was identified by her microchip when she turned up near home in early 2013, two months after she vanished.
